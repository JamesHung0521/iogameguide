🎮 Hexanaut.io

Hexanaut.io Guide: Master Territory Capture & King Strategy

🎮 Play Hexanaut.io Now
By Alex Rivera, Senior Editor • Updated: July 30, 2026 • ⏱️ 12 min read

The Hex That Hooked Me

I'll be honest — when I first loaded Hexanaut.io, I figured it was just another Paper.io clone with a cosmetic hexagonal reskin. Thirty minutes later, I was leaning forward in my chair, sweating a King defense with twelve seconds on the timer, watching three players converge on my border from different angles. I lost that match. I queued again immediately.

That's the thing about Hexanaut.io. The core concept — leave your territory, draw a trail, loop back, capture everything inside — is immediately legible to anyone who's touched a territory .io game before. But the execution adds layers that crept up on me. The hexagonal grid changes how loops feel. The totem system turns the map into a resource board. And the King mechanic, with its no-respawn death rule, creates stakes that most browser games can't touch.

Exodragon Games built this. Same studio behind Defly.io, which I've also spent an embarrassing number of hours on. They clearly understand something about .io game design that a lot of developers don't: simplicity in mechanics is not the same as simplicity in strategy. Hexanaut.io's rules fit in a paragraph. Its decision tree doesn't fit in a spreadsheet.

The game has been live since March 2022, and according to Coolmath Games — where it's one of the most-played titles — it's racked up over 930,000 user ratings averaging 4.7 out of 5. That's not a flash-in-the-pan hit. That's a game with legs.

So here's what I've learned after a few hundred matches, a lot of dead ends, and some genuinely brilliant moments of play I wish I'd recorded. This isn't a bullet-point list of "tips." It's a field guide.

Editor's Note: I came to Hexanaut.io from a Paper.io background, expecting to transfer my skills directly. They transferred — sort of. But the hex grid and totem system forced me to unlearn habits that were actively getting me killed. If you're making the same jump, give yourself twenty matches to recalibrate before judging your performance.

Booting Up: Controls and First Moves

Hexanaut.io runs entirely in your browser. No download, no account required to play — though I'd recommend creating one if you want to track stats, earn coins, and climb the ranked ladder. The game also has an Android app if you prefer mobile.

The controls are minimal by design:

InputAction
Mouse movementCharacter aims toward cursor direction
WASD / Arrow keysAlternative movement control
Spacebar / ClickActivate items (PRO mode)
Touch & drag (mobile)Full mobile support with virtual joystick option
EscPause and check leaderboard

Mouse control is the way to go on desktop. Your character follows the cursor smoothly, and the hexagonal grid means you're moving in six directions rather than four — the mouse handles that nuance far better than keyboard keys. That said, some players I've talked to in the community prefer WASD for precision during tight combat moments. Try both. There's no wrong answer.

One setting worth changing before your first real match: go into Settings and look for the movement options. The virtual joystick toggle is genuinely better on mobile, and on desktop, enabling smoother turning can help you execute the quick direction changes that tail-cutting combat demands.

Your first match will probably last about ninety seconds. That's fine. Everyone's first match does. Spawn, make a few small loops near your starting hex, get ambitious, overextend, and get sliced. The loop of failure and improvement in Hexanaut.io is fast — you're back in a new match within seconds. Use those early games to feel out the movement and get comfortable with how trails work.

The Trail Economy: How Territory Actually Works

Let's get specific about what's actually happening when you play, because understanding the mechanics at a granular level is what separates consistent winners from everyone else.

When you're standing on your own colored hexagons, you're invincible. No one can touch you. The moment you cross your border, your character starts laying down a visible trail. That trail is your lifeline and your vulnerability simultaneously. To capture new territory, you draw a path through unclaimed or enemy hexes and then return to any part of your own territory. The moment you reconnect, every hexagon enclosed within the shape you drew flips to your color.

Simple enough. Here's where it gets brutal: while you're outside your borders, any opponent who touches your trail kills you instantly. No grace period, no health bar, no second chance. You're dead, your territory goes neutral, and someone else gets to scoop it up.

The same rule applies in reverse, of course. Spot an enemy with a long, exposed trail? Cut across it and they're gone. Their territory opens up for the taking. This creates a constant tension between greed and caution. Every hexagon you try to claim is a gamble — the bigger the loop, the bigger the payoff, but also the longer your trail sits exposed.

There are a couple of edge cases worth knowing:

If two players collide head-to-head outside both their territories, both die. On your own territory, you're always safe — an enemy crashing into you on your land does nothing to you. This means you can use your border as a weapon: bait an enemy to chase you, then duck inside your territory at the last second. They either pull back or crash into your border helplessly.

And here's a detail that took me an embarrassing number of matches to internalize: the map shrinks over time. The arena contracts, forcing players closer together and making large-scale expansion impossible. If you're still hugging the edges when the shrink kicks in, you're going to get cornered. Start migrating toward the center earlier than feels comfortable.

Five Monuments: Reading the Totem Board

Totems are the thing that elevates Hexanaut.io above a pure reflex contest. Five types of totem structures are scattered across the map, and capturing one (by enclosing it within your territory) grants a persistent buff for as long as you hold it. They turn the map from an empty grid into a resource board — suddenly it matters where you expand, not just how much.

Here's my breakdown of each, in rough order of how much I prioritize them:

⚡ Speed Totem

Grants +5% movement speed, and it's stackable. Grab three or four of these and you become genuinely frightening — faster than almost anyone on the map. Speed wins chases, escapes, and races to close loops. I prioritize these above everything else in the first two minutes of a match. A player with stacked Speed Totems can close a risky loop that would kill a normal-speed player.

🕵️ Spy Dish

Reveals all opponent territories on your minimap. Early game, this is nice-to-have. Late game, it's essential. Once you're controlling 15%+ of the map, you physically cannot watch every border simultaneously. The Spy Dish turns guesswork into information. Without it, you're reacting to incursions after they've already started. With it, you can intercept before the enemy finishes their loop.

🌐 Spreading Totem

The only totem that captures territory for you. Once claimed, it fires out sequential lasers that auto-claim nearby hexagons one by one. In the first sixty seconds of a match, this is arguably the strongest totem — it builds your foundation while you're free to focus on other things. In the late game, its value drops since the hexes it grabs are usually near your existing borders.

🚪 Teleport Gate

Instantly transports you across your territory to another gate. The value scales with territory size. When you're controlling 10% of the map, it's a convenience. When you're King with 25%, it's your primary defensive tool — someone attacks your eastern border, you're there in a blink. I've won King defenses purely because a Teleport Gate let me respond to a border breach before the enemy finished their loop.

🕸️ Slowing Totem

Creates a cloudy zone that drastically slows any enemy who enters it. You're unaffected. This is your trap card. Position it near a chokepoint on your border, and when an aggressive player rushes in to cut your trail, they hit the slow field and become an easy target. The flip side: never enter an enemy's Slowing Totem zone unless you have a Shield active. You'll be a sitting duck.

Pro Insight: Totem priority isn't fixed — it shifts with match phase. Spreading and Speed dominate the opening. Spy Dish and Teleport become critical once you're approaching King territory. Slowing Totems are your best friend during the King defense. Think of totem acquisition as a phased strategy, not a shopping list.

The Crown Gambit: From Zero to King

Reaching 20% map control and claiming the King title is the central objective of every Hexanaut.io match. The path there is different every time — it depends on spawn location, totem placement, server population, and how aggressive your neighbors are. But there are principles that hold across matches.

Phase one: paint, don't gamble. The first two minutes should be small, tight loops around your spawn. Think of it like painting a wall — overlapping strokes, building coverage gradually. Each loop should take you maybe five to eight seconds outside your territory. Short trails mean short exposure windows. The goal is to build a contiguous block of maybe 5-8% of the map before you start thinking bigger.

I see too many players attempt a massive sweeping loop on their first or second venture out. It almost never works. Someone spots the long trail, cuts it, and you're back to zero. Patience is not optional in the early game — it's the strategy.

Phase two: totem-driven expansion. Once you have a solid base, start expanding toward totems. The Spreading Totem is your accelerator here — claim it and let it passively grab hexes while you make manual loops elsewhere. Grab Speed Totems whenever they're on your path. This is also when the "peninsula and bridge" technique shines: extend two protrusions from your territory, then connect them with a single horizontal loop, capturing a large triangle of hexes in one move. It's efficient and the trail length is manageable.

Phase three: the King moment. Hitting 20% is anticlimactic in the best way. A banner appears. You get a speed bonus and full minimap visibility. And then reality sets in.

Because here's what changes the instant you become King: every other player on the server can now see your exact position on their minimap. You're not just the biggest target — you're a marked target. And there's a far more sinister rule: once a King is crowned, any player who dies cannot respawn. They're out of the match permanently. This means aggressive players have a strong incentive to start hunting, because every elimination permanently shrinks the competition.

Holding the Throne: Surviving the King Timer

You're King. A countdown timer starts — typically two minutes — and you need to keep your territory above 20% for the entire duration. If you drop below the threshold, you lose the crown. If you survive the timer, you win the match.

Everything about your playstyle should change at this point. Stop expanding aggressively. I mean it. The instinct to push for 25%, 30% territory is strong, and it will get you killed. Every time you leave your borders to grab more hexes, you're exposing your trail to players who know exactly where you are.

Instead, shift to defense. Here's what that looks like in practice:

Patrol your borders. Stay inside your territory and move along the edges, watching for enemies approaching. If someone starts drawing a loop into your land, you have two options: intercept them on your territory (where you're safe and they're not) or wait for them to overextend and cut their trail when they try to return to their own base.

If you have a Teleport Gate, this is its moment. Park yourself near the center of your territory, watch the minimap for border incursions, and teleport to whichever gate is closest to the threat. I once defended a King timer for the full two minutes without leaving my territory once — just teleporting between three gates, intercepting four separate incursions. It was the most stressful two minutes of my Hexanaut.io career, and it worked because I had the Spy Dish active and could see everything coming.

If enemies are particularly aggressive and breaching multiple borders simultaneously, consider sacrificing peripheral territory. You don't need to hold every hexagon — you need to stay above 20%. Let them take the edges. Consolidate around your core. A smaller, defensible territory above 20% beats a sprawling empire that you can't protect.

Editor's Note: The single most common way I see Kings lose is panic-expansion. They drop to 19% after an enemy captures a chunk of their border, and instead of retaking that specific area, they panic and launch a big loop into neutral territory to recover percentage. That loop gets cut, they die, and the match is over. When your percentage dips, retake lost ground with small, safe loops near your border. Never chase percentage with a long trail.

Territory Games Under the Microscope: Hexanaut vs Paper.io vs Splix.io

If you've played territory .io games, you've probably played Paper.io. It's the genre's progenitor — a square-grid land grab where you draw rectangles, close loops, and try not to get your tail cut. Splix.io took the same concept and refined it with a blockier aesthetic and smoother netcode. Hexanaut.io takes it in a different direction entirely. Let me break down how they actually compare.

Grid shape matters more than you'd think. Paper.io and Splix.io use square grids. Movement is in four directions, loops are rectangular, and territory borders follow clean right angles. Hexanaut.io's hexagonal grid adds two more movement axes. Loops can be more organic, borders are harder to seal completely, and the geometry of "what's enclosed" is less intuitive. It sounds minor, but it changes the feel of every interaction. Cutting a trail on a hex grid requires different spatial reasoning than on a square grid.

Totems change the strategic layer entirely. Neither Paper.io nor Splix.io has anything equivalent. In those games, the map is uniform — every tile is the same. In Hexanaut.io, totems create hotspots. Players fight over them, route their expansion toward them, and use them as defensive anchors. The map isn't just space — it's space with strategic value attached. This is the single biggest differentiator, and it's why I keep coming back to Hexanaut.io when I've gotten bored of the other two.

The King mechanic creates stakes that Paper.io can't match. Paper.io is essentially an endless score chase. You play until you die, and your score is your territory percentage at time of death. There's no win condition. Splix.io is similar — it's about leaderboard position, not match victory. Hexanaut.io has an actual win condition: become King, hold for the timer, win the match. And the no-respawn rule during King phase means late-game decisions carry real weight. You're not just playing for a higher score — you're playing to win or lose.

3D presentation. Hexanaut.io's 3D rendering gives it a visual polish that Paper.io's flat 2D and Splix.io's minimalist style can't match. It's not just cosmetic — the 3D perspective affects how you perceive distance and trail length. You need to be more aware of what's off-screen because the camera angle can make trails look shorter than they are.

Match length and pacing. Paper.io sessions can drag on indefinitely. Splix.io matches are similar. Hexanaut.io's shrinking arena and King timer create natural match arcs — most games resolve in 5-10 minutes. The shrinking arena is borrowed from battle royale design, and it works. It forces confrontation and prevents stalemates.

My verdict? Paper.io is the genre's foundation — worth playing for historical context. Splix.io is the cleaner, faster alternative if you want pure territory capture without extras. Hexanaut.io is the deepest of the three, the one with the most strategic variety, and the one I'd recommend to anyone who's already familiar with the basic formula and wants something with more teeth.

PRO FFA: When Airdrops Change the Game

Standard FFA is Hexanaut.io at its purest — territory, trails, totems, nothing else. PRO FFA throws all of that into a blender by adding airdropped combat items that fundamentally change how fights work. If you've gotten comfortable in regular FFA and want to be challenged again, this is where you go.

In PRO FFA, airdrops periodically land on your territory. Walking over one gives you a random item that you activate with Spacebar or Click. Each item is single-use, and timing is everything. Here's the full arsenal:

ItemEffect
🔫 CannonFire bullets that slice through opponent heads from distance
✂️ ScissorsLaunch projectiles to cut enemy tails remotely
💣 Capture GrenadeInstantly claim territory in an area with explosive capture
⚡ Speed CellTemporary massive speed burst for escapes or aggression
🧱 Brick WallDeploy impassable barriers to trap or protect territory
🛡️ ShieldBecome invincible against all collisions and tail cuts
🌿 Capture RakeDramatically increase territory gain per movement step

These items sound straightforward on paper, but the strategic depth comes from timing and combination. A Shield lets you close a massive, risky loop that would normally be suicide. A Cannon lets you snipe a King from range without ever leaving your territory. Scissors turn trail-cutting into a ranged weapon — you don't need to physically cross someone's trail anymore, you just need line of sight.

The Brick Wall is sneakier than it looks. Drop it in front of an enemy who's chasing your trail, and they slam into an invisible barrier. Use it to seal off a chokepoint on your border. Or — and this is devious — place it behind an enemy who's venturing into your territory, cutting off their retreat path. They're stuck inside your borders, where you're invincible and they're not.

My recommendation for PRO FFA: don't hoard items. The temptation to save everything for "the right moment" means you end up never using them at all. If you have a Speed Cell and you're about to close a risky loop, pop it. If you have a Capture Grenade and there's a juicy cluster of neutral hexes nearby, throw it. Items are renewable — more airdrops are always coming. The player who uses items aggressively and consistently will outperform the one who saves them for a perfect moment that never arrives.

PRO FFA also features increased base speed, which compresses decision-making windows. Everything happens faster. Trails get cut quicker, loops close quicker, and the margin for error shrinks. It's Hexanaut.io on hard mode, and it's where I've had some of my most intense matches.

The Ranked Grind: Copper to Grandmaster

Hexanaut.io's ranking system gives you a progression track beyond individual matches. You earn XP from wins, eliminations, and territory captured, climbing through eight tiers:

What I appreciate about this system is that XP isn't just from winning — you earn it from slices and territory too. This means even matches where you don't win still contribute to progression, as long as you're playing well. It keeps the grind from feeling punishing.

The jump from Gold to Platinum is where I noticed the skill floor rising sharply. Players at Platinum and above consistently use totems strategically, watch their minimaps, and don't overextend. At Diamond and above, you start seeing players who can bait and counter-bait — they'll fake an aggressive push to draw you out, then circle around to cut your trail while you're committed. It's genuinely high-level play.

War Stories from the Server

One of the things that keeps Hexanaut.io engaging is the community. The game has a dedicated player base that's active across Discord, Reddit, and various .io game forums. I've picked up more strategy from community discussions than from any amount of solo play.

There's a recurring discussion in the community about the "edge strategy" — spawning at the map edge and building inward versus spawning centrally and building outward. Players who favor the edge argue that it eliminates one direction of attack entirely — no one can come at you from behind the map boundary. Central players argue that edge spawns limit your expansion options and leave you vulnerable when the arena shrinks.

I've tried both extensively. Edge starts are safer in the early game, no question. But they become a liability once the arena starts contracting, because you're being pushed inward with no room to maneuver. My current approach: start at the edge if I spawn there, build a solid base, but begin migrating toward the center well before the first arena shrink. It's the best of both worlds — early safety, late-game positioning.

A player I spoke with on Discord — goes by the handle HexReaper — described a tactic I'd never considered: intentionally letting your territory get "bitten" at a specific border to lure enemies into a false sense of security, then ambushing them with a Teleport Gate when they push deeper. "The best defense isn't holding every border," they told me. "It's making the enemy think they're winning. Then you collapse on them from a direction they forgot about." That's the kind of thinking that separates Gold from Diamond.

Another community discussion I found valuable centers on Duo mode coordination. The consensus is that the most effective Duo strategy is role specialization: one player focuses on aggressive expansion while the other maintains a defensive perimeter. The defender stays inside shared territory, watching borders and intercepting incursions, while the expander takes calculated risks knowing someone has their back. When the expander gets into trouble, the defender can venture out to cut the pursuer's trail. It requires communication, but when it works, it's devastating.

The game's developer, Exodragon Games, has been consistent with updates. The ranking system, cosmetic skins, and Duo mode were all added post-launch based on community feedback. The Weekly mode — which rotates special rules — is a direct response to players asking for variety. An engaged developer makes a big difference in a game's longevity, and Hexanaut.io has benefited from that.

The Slice List: How Not to Get Yourself Killed

I've made every mistake on this list. Some of them multiple times. Learn from my failures:

  1. The greedy first loop. You spawn, see open territory, and immediately try to claim a quarter of the map in one sweeping arc. Someone cuts your trail at the halfway point. You're dead in thirty seconds. Start small. Build a base. Expand from strength, not hope.
  2. Forgetting to look behind you. The minimap exists for a reason. The most common death in Hexanaut.io isn't from the player in front of you — it's from the one who slipped into your blind spot while you were focused on closing a loop. Glance at the minimap before you commit to any trail.
  3. Totem tunnel vision. You spot a Speed Totem across open territory and bee-line for it, ignoring the three players between you and it. You get cut on the way there. Totems are valuable, but they're not worth dying for. Route toward them through your own territory whenever possible.
  4. Panic expansion as King. I covered this in the King defense section, but it deserves repeating because it's the #1 King death cause. You drop below 20%, panic, and launch a Hail Mary loop. Don't. Retake lost ground methodically with short, safe loops.
  5. Ignoring the arena shrink. The map doesn't stay the same size. If you're still building at the edges when the arena contracts, you'll lose territory passively and get squeezed into a shrinking space with every other player. Start moving toward center early — uncomfortably early.
  6. Entering enemy Slowing Totem zones. This one's simple: if you see a cloudy area near an enemy's border, that's a trap. Don't enter it without a Shield active. You'll be moving at a crawl while the enemy circles you at full speed.
  7. Forgetting that head-on collisions kill both players. Sometimes ramming into an enemy head-first feels like a good trade. It rarely is. You lose your territory, your totems, and your match position just to take out one opponent. Only do this if you're already in a hopeless position and want to take someone down with you.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you win in Hexanaut.io?

Capture at least 20% of the total map to become the Hexanaut King. Once you achieve King status, you must maintain that territory level for the full countdown timer. If you succeed, you win the match. Alternatively, if you're the King and every other player is eliminated, you also win.

What happens if I die while someone else is King?

You cannot respawn. If another player holds the King title when you're eliminated, you're out of the match permanently. This makes King battles incredibly high-stakes — the longer someone holds the crown, the fewer opponents remain.

What are the 5 totems?

Speed Totem (+5% movement speed, stackable), Spreading Totem (auto-captures nearby hexagons), Teleport Gate (instant travel across your territory), Slowing Totem (creates a zone that slows enemies), and Spy Dish (reveals all territories on minimap). Each persists as long as you control the territory containing it.

Is Hexanaut.io free?

Yes. Hexanaut.io is completely free to play in your web browser. No downloads, no sign-ups required — though creating a free account lets you save progress, track stats, and unlock cosmetic skins. It's also available as an Android app.

Can I play Hexanaut.io on mobile?

Absolutely. The game is fully optimized for mobile with touch controls and an optional virtual joystick. It runs well on Chromebooks too, which is why it's popular in school settings.

What's the difference between FFA and PRO FFA?

PRO FFA adds airdropped combat items — Cannon, Scissors, Capture Grenade, Speed Cell, Brick Wall, Shield, and Capture Rake — and increases base movement speed. It's a faster, more aggressive mode that rewards item management alongside territory strategy.

Who made Hexanaut.io?

Hexanaut.io was developed by Exodragon Games, the same studio behind Defly.io. They specialize in multiplayer .io browser games with 3D graphics.

How long does a typical match last?

Most matches last 5-10 minutes. Aggressive early expansion can get you to King status in under 3 minutes, but the defense phase adds time. The shrinking arena ensures matches don't drag on indefinitely.

Is there a Duo mode?

Yes. Duo mode lets you team up with a partner to share territory and totem bonuses. It's available in both standard and PRO variants, and effective coordination — one expanding, one defending — is extremely powerful.
