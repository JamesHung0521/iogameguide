Starve.io Guide: Survival, Crafting & Biome Mastery

By Alex Rivera, Senior Editor•Updated: 2026-07-30•15 min read

🎮 Play Starve.io Now

Spawn Point: My First Death and What It Taught Me

I spawned at dusk. No tools, no food, no idea what I was doing. Within ninety seconds, a wolf caught me halfway between a berry bush and a tree. My health bar vanished before I even understood the controls.

That was my introduction to Starve.io, and honestly, it's the perfect one. This game does not hold your hand. It drops you into a 2D wilderness with nothing but bare fists and a vague instinct to survive, then lets the environment teach you — brutally — what you did wrong. And you will do a lot of things wrong.

Starve.io is a browser-based multiplayer survival game from French developer LapaMauve. Think Minecraft's resource gathering and crafting, compressed into a top-down .io format with persistent progression, seasonal resets, and a player-vs-player endgame that gets surprisingly political. You gather wood, stone, and food. You craft tools, build bases, and fight off wolves, spiders, and eventually dragons. You die. You respawn. You try again, smarter.

What hooked me — and what keeps a dedicated community coming back season after season — is how every mechanic interlocks. Hunger drives you to hunt. Hunting drives you to craft weapons. Weapons drive you to mine better materials. Better materials drive you to explore dangerous biomes. Dangerous biomes force you to build better defenses. It's a tight, elegant survival loop that rewards planning and punishes complacency in equal measure.

Editor's Note: I've sunk over 300 hours into Starve.io across four seasons. I've been a farmer, a builder, a lone wolf survivor, and — I'll admit it — a savage who raided other players' bases. This guide distills everything I've learned, plus strategies from the community wiki, veteran players on the official Discord, and some hard-won lessons from deaths I'd rather not relive.

The Three Bars That Rule Everything

Three meters determine whether you live or die in Starve.io: Health, Hunger, and Temperature. A fourth — Hydration — joins the party once you start spending extended time near water or in hot biomes. Every survival decision you make is ultimately about keeping these bars from hitting zero.

Hunger is the clock that's always ticking. It depletes at 0.56 units per second, and when it empties, your health starts draining. Berries are your day-one lifeline — just hit berry bushes to collect them. But berries restore only 5 hunger each, so you'll burn through them fast. The real solution is cooked meat: wolves and spiders drop raw meat, and cooking it over a campfire more than doubles its nutritional value. Later on, you can farm pumpkins and garlic, which are far more efficient per plot. The community wiki's farming data shows pumpkins as the most calorie-dense crop, with garlic as a close second that also provides cold resistance.

Temperature is the bar that kills new players faster than anything else. During the day in the Forest biome, you're fine. At night, the temperature drops fast, and if it hits zero, you start losing health — slowly at first, then accelerating. A campfire is your single most important early-game craft. Not a sword. Not a pickaxe. A campfire. You can gather resources with your fists. You cannot punch the cold.

I learned this the hard way on my third spawn. I'd gathered enough wood for a pickaxe and was sprinting toward a stone deposit when the sky darkened. "I'll be quick," I told myself. I was not quick. By the time I realized I was freezing, my health was at 30%. I died ten steps from another player's campfire.

Hydration matters once you're near water or in the Desert biome, where thirst depletes faster. Standing near a water source refills it automatically, but wading in too long costs you temperature and oxygen. A bottle (crafted from sand at a workbench) lets you carry water on the go — essential for long expeditions.

📋 Quick Reference: Survival Bars

Health (green): Damaged by mobs, freezing, starving, and drowning. Regenerates slowly when fed and warm.
Hunger (red): Drops 0.56/sec constantly. Berries → cooked meat → farmed crops. AFK for 1 hour? Bring 216 berries or 22 cakes.
Temperature (blue-white): Drops at night and in Winter biome. Campfire, torch, coat, or fur hat keep it stable.
Hydration (blue): Depletes faster in Desert/Lava. Stand near water or carry a bottle.

Stone Age to Dragon Fire: The Crafting Ladder

The crafting progression in Starve.io follows a strict material hierarchy: Wood → Stone → Gold → Diamond → Amethyst → Reidite → Dragon. Each tier is locked behind the biome where its materials spawn, which means your crafting journey doubles as an exploration roadmap.

Here's the thing that trips up new players: you can't skip tiers. A stone pickaxe can mine gold, but only a gold pickaxe can mine diamonds. Only a diamond pickaxe can mine amethyst. This creates a natural pacing that prevents rushing, but it also means a well-timed tier upgrade can dramatically shift your power level in a single session.

The first ten minutes are non-negotiable. Punch a tree for 10 wood. Craft a wooden pickaxe. Mine stone. Upgrade to stone tools. Kill a wolf with your stone sword for meat. Cook it. Build a campfire. This sequence — which I've executed probably 200 times across seasons — takes about five minutes if you're efficient, and it's the difference between surviving your first night and respawning with nothing.

One of the longest-running debates in the Starve.io community is swords versus spears. Swords deal more damage per hit, making them better for grinding mobs and most PvP situations. Spears have longer range, which means you can hit enemies from outside their attack radius — crucial for fighting fast mobs like wolves, and for team PvP where first-hit advantage matters. Most veteran players I've talked to carry both: a sword for PvE efficiency and a spear for PvP reach. Bows exist too, but they're expensive — the Dragon Bow requires killing some of the hardest mobs in the game, including the Sandworm, and crafting 200+ of multiple resources. When a bow hits, though, it hits hard.

Pro Insight: The Machete is an underrated early-game tool. It doesn't deal great damage, but it doubles the drops from every mob you kill. If you're farming wolves for the fur and thread needed to craft a Bag (essential for inventory expansion), a machete turns 9 wolves into the equivalent of 18. The time investment pays for itself within a single hunting session.

The crafting tree also includes structural items — walls, doors, roofs, workbenches, campfires, chests, bridges, spikes, and towers — each available in the same tier progression. A wooden wall stops a wolf. A diamond wall stops most players. A Reidite wall is what you build when you're serious about defending your base against endgame raiders.

Where You Build Determines How Long You Live

Base location in Starve.io is not a casual decision. It's the single most important strategic choice you'll make, and it determines your entire playstyle for the rest of the season.

The community wiki breaks base locations into several categories, and after trying all of them, I largely agree with their assessments:

The Forest is where everyone starts, and it's the most contested zone. Resources are abundant — wood, stone, gold, berries, and basic mob drops are everywhere. But that abundance attracts other players, including the ones who want to take your stuff. If you build here, build small, build hidden, and build fast. The lower-right corner of the map tends to be less trafficked, which makes it a decent compromise.

The Beach/Forest edge gives you automatic hydration refill and access to fishing, but it's even more visible than the deep forest. I'd only recommend this for larger teams who can defend a visible position.

The Winter biome is where things get interesting. It's large, sparsely populated, and the cold naturally deters casual raiders. If you have proper cold protection (a coat or better), you can build a base here that most players will never find. The downside: winter mobs can spawn inside your walls, and blizzards deal damage every 4 seconds unless you have a roof.

The Ocean is the meta choice for serious survivors. Building a base on the water, connected to land by bridges, creates a natural moat. Pirates are rare, and most players won't bother swimming out to raid you. The catch? You need approximately 20,000 wood for the bridges alone, plus walls robust enough to survive the Kraken that prowls the deep water. It's a massive investment, but it's the closest thing to a safe AFK spot in the game.

The Desert and Lava biomes are generally terrible for bases. Sandworms in the Desert can destroy bridges and walls. Lava Dragons in the Lava biome chew through even Reidite walls. Don't build here unless you have a very specific reason.

🏗️ Base Building Fundamental

Place your workbench against a resource deposit. It serves double duty: crafting station and defensive wall. Add a roof immediately — it prevents mobs from spawning inside your base and provides warmth in cold biomes. Pin your location on the map (left-click your orange dot) so you can find your way back after dying.

The Biome Roadmap: Forest to Fire

The map in Starve.io is structured as concentric rings of increasing danger. You spawn in the Forest, push north into the Winter, discover the Desert to the east, and eventually reach the Lava biome at the map's edge. Each ring gates progression through its resources, its mobs, and its environmental hazards.

Forest is your home. Wood, stone, gold, berries, and the mobs that drop essential early-game materials: wolves (fur, thread, meat), spiders (silk, meat), rabbits (meat), and boars (meat, leather). Crabs and king crabs spawn near water — crabs are fast and hit hard, so bait them at range and hit them every 4-5 movements. The Forest also contains the game's only berry bushes, making it the de facto farming zone for the first several days.

Winter is where the game opens up. Diamonds are exclusive to this biome — you need a gold pickaxe to mine them, and you need earmuffs or a coat to survive the cold long enough to do so. The Winter cave is the most valuable single location on the map: it contains both diamonds and amethyst in the same area, plus maze-like walls made of depleted stone. It's also a PvP hotspot. Every player progressing through Chrono Quests passes through here, and the ones hunting for kills know it. Go armed, go with a team if possible, and watch your temperature bar constantly.

Winter mobs are a significant step up. Polar bears are slow but hit like trucks — circle a campfire to stay warm while fighting, hitting once or twice per revolution. Winter foxes are faster and more dangerous. Mammoths deal 70 damage per hit — three hits without armor and you're dead. But mammoth fur is essential for crafting the fur hat, which provides temperature stability in cold biomes. The risk-reward calculation here is the heart of Starve.io's design.

Desert is governed by sandstorms that deal continuous damage unless you're under a roof or wearing a cap or turban. Sandworms patrol the area, and they can destroy any structure — walls, bridges, everything. What the Desert offers in return: Emeralds, one of the game's rarest resources, used in advanced crafting and the Emerald Machine that generates score over time.

Lava is the endgame biome. The temperature is lethally hot regardless of time of day — you'll need ice from the Winter biome to survive. Lava Monsters and Lava Dragons spawn here, and the Reidite Sword (craftable only from Reidite found in this biome) is the most powerful melee weapon in the game, dealing bonus damage against Lava Monsters specifically. This is where the top-tier players spend their late seasons, fighting over the most valuable real estate on the map.

When Players Become Predators: PvP and the Social Economy

Here's where Starve.io separates itself from every other survival .io game I've played: the PvP isn't just combat. It's a social ecosystem with its own unwritten rules, its own etiquette, and its own controversies.

The community has a word for players who attack others unprovoked: "savages." Being a savage is the most controversial playstyle in the game. You gain points fast — kills are worth significant score — but you make enemies faster. The community Discord is full of threads calling out specific savages, organizing retaliatory raids, and debating whether the playstyle is legitimate or toxic. It's genuinely fascinating to watch.

For most players, the smarter approach is what the community calls "defensive PvP." Build a strong base. Equip good gear. Make it clear that raiding you isn't worth the trouble. A player in full diamond or amethyst gear, standing behind diamond walls, with spike traps and a tower — that's a target that most savages will skip in favor of easier prey.

The unwritten rules — as I've observed them across multiple seasons — roughly break down like this: Don't attack players with visibly lower-tier gear. Don't destroy bases that are clearly AFK (their owners will remember). Help new players when you can — the community is small enough that reputation matters. And if you do go savage, own it. Players respect honesty more than excuses.

I ran into a player last season who'd built a hotel — a large, open structure with multiple campfires, a water source, and food storage, explicitly designed as a rest stop for travelers. He'd been running it for three weeks. Other players brought him materials to expand it. A team of savages tried to raid it once, and half the server showed up to defend it. That's the kind of emergent social dynamic that Starve.io's design makes possible, and it's why the game has held my attention for hundreds of hours.

Team play changes everything. Team Mode uses Totems to formally bind players together, but even in Normal Mode, informal alliances form constantly. A team of three can have one player farming, one gathering resources, and one guarding the base — a division of labor that dramatically increases survival time. In PvP, coordinated teams can overwhelm even well-geared solo players through sheer numbers and positioning.

The taming system adds another layer. You can tame Baby Mammoths (faster than sleds in snow), Hawks (aerial scouts), Boars, King Crabs, and even Baby Dragons. A tamed creature serves as both a mount and a combat companion. Baby Mammoths are the safest taming target — they're harmless unless attacked first — and they're essential for efficient Winter biome travel.

Starve.io vs Minecraft: What's Actually Different

People compare Starve.io to Minecraft constantly, and the comparison makes sense on the surface. Both games involve punching trees, crafting tools, surviving the night, and building bases. But the actual experience of playing them is radically different, and understanding those differences is key to enjoying Starve.io on its own terms.

Scale and pace. Minecraft is a 3D sandbox where you can spend hours building elaborate structures with no pressure. Starve.io compresses survival into a 2D top-down format where every second outside your base at night is a calculated risk. The urgency is higher, the death penalty stiffer (you drop everything on death in most modes), and the pace is faster. A Starve.io session can go from "I'm doing great" to "I'm dead" in about fifteen seconds if you get careless.

PvP is the endgame, not an afterthought. In Minecraft, PvP servers exist but they're a subset of the game. In Starve.io, PvP is baked into the core loop. Every player you encounter is a potential threat, potential ally, or potential target. The risk of running into another player adds tension to every expedition outside your base. Minecraft doesn't have this — even on survival servers, encounters are relatively rare and the world is large enough to avoid people.

Seasonal resets change the psychology. Minecraft worlds are persistent — what you build lasts forever (or until you stop playing). Starve.io seasons reset everything: levels, bases, resources. This creates a cyclical meta where early-season land grabs, mid-season resource accumulation, and late-season PvP escalation each have distinct rhythms. It also means that losing everything isn't catastrophic — it's the natural end of a cycle. New season, fresh start.

Crafting depth vs breadth. Minecraft has thousands of items and recipes across mods. Starve.io has a focused, curated crafting tree where every item serves a clear purpose. There's no "diamond hoe" that nobody uses. Every tier of every tool type is a meaningful upgrade. This focus makes progression feel more intentional — when you craft a Diamond Sword, you feel the power increase immediately.

Other survival .io games like Moomoo.io and Zombs.io share Starve.io's top-down format, but they lack the biome variety, crafting depth, and taming system. Starve.io sits in a sweet spot: deep enough to satisfy Minecraft veterans, accessible enough for .io game players, and fast enough for browser gaming sessions.

The Community Is Half the Game

I've played a lot of browser games, and most of them have communities that range from indifferent to actively hostile. Starve.io is different. The community is small — small enough that you start recognizing usernames across seasons — but it's engaged, knowledgeable, and surprisingly collaborative.

The Starve.io Pro Wiki on Fandom is the closest thing the game has to a bible. It contains detailed strategy guides, crafting recipes, mob stats, and biome maps, all maintained by dedicated community members. The "Road to Reidite" guide — which walks you through getting endgame tools from a fresh spawn — is required reading for any serious player. The farming data tables, which calculate food-per-second for every crop type, have directly informed my farming strategy for two seasons straight.

The official Discord is where the living discussion happens. Strategy debates, base design showcases, savage callouts, team recruitment, and seasonal meta analysis all happen in real time. I've picked up more practical tips from Discord conversations than from any written guide — there's something about watching a veteran player explain how they kite a Mammoth in real-time that just doesn't translate to text.

Content creators keep the game visible. YouTube has a healthy collection of Starve.io content: speedruns from fresh spawn to Reidite gear, base tour videos, PvP highlight compilations, and tutorial walkthroughs. The Japanese wiki community in particular has produced incredibly detailed mob strategy guides — their Mammoth and Dragon breakdowns are the most thorough I've seen anywhere.

One thing I appreciate about the community is how it handles the tension between PvE and PvP players. Both playstyles are valid, both have their advocates, and the community has largely settled on a live-and-let-live approach — with the understanding that if you build in a high-traffic area with weak walls, you're accepting the risk. It's an organic social contract that emerged from player behavior rather than developer enforcement, and it works remarkably well.

Editor's Note: If you're new to Starve.io, join the Discord before you start your first serious session. Having a place to ask questions — "What do I do with thread?" "How do I survive the first blizzard?" "Why did that guy in a diamond helmet just destroy my entire base?" — will save you hours of trial and error. The community is genuinely welcoming to newcomers who show up willing to learn.

The First Night Playbook

Everything above is context. Here's the actual sequence of actions that will get you past your first night, compiled from personal experience, the community wiki, and conversations with veteran players:

If you spawn during the day: Punch the nearest tree until you have 10 wood. Craft a wooden pickaxe immediately. Find a stone deposit and mine 50 stone. Craft a stone pickaxe and a stone sword. Kill wolves for meat — lure them close, hit their nose, back away, repeat. Collect berries from every bush you pass. Before the sky darkens, build a campfire. Then build walls around it. Then eat.

If you spawn at night: You have maybe 30 seconds before the cold hits. Find another player's campfire and stick close to it — most players won't attack you on night one, and the alternative is death. If no campfire is nearby, gather 10 wood in the dark, craft a pickaxe, mine 5 stone, and build your own fire. Prioritize warmth over everything. You can gather resources at dawn.

Day two priorities: Craft a workbench. Build a bag (requires wolf fur and spider thread — kill 5-9 wolves and 3-5 spiders, fewer if you have a machete). Stockpile cooked meat. Scout for a base location near water and multiple resource nodes. Pin it on your map. Start building walls.

Day three through five: Upgrade to gold tools. Craft a coat or earmuffs for cold protection. Stockpile 200+ gold, 100+ stone, and 3-4 campfires. Prepare for the Winter expedition — check the map for blizzard warnings before you go. A blizzard midway through your journey is a death sentence without proper preparation.

The Winter push: Travel north with gold pickaxe, cold protection, campfires, and food. Don't stop to hunt arctic mobs on your first trip — get to the diamond deposits, mine what you can, and get out. Once you have diamond tools, the game fundamentally changes. You can build diamond walls, craft diamond weapons, and survive Winter indefinitely.

❓ FAQ

Is Starve.io free to play?

Yes, Starve.io is completely free to play in your web browser. There are no downloads required. Simply visit starve.io and start playing.

What game modes are available?

Starve.io offers Normal Mode, Zombie Mode, Vampire Mode, Experimental Mode, Team Mode, and Community Modes. Experimental servers let you earn golden bread but don't save scores to the official leaderboard. Private servers support custom game types like Murder Party and Hunger Games modes.

How do I survive the first night?

Immediately gather 10 wood for a pickaxe and berries for food. Find stone for basic tools. Build a campfire before dark — this is your most critical action. Stay near the fire and avoid hostile mobs. If you spawn just before dark, head to the river and look for other players' campfires.

Can I play with friends?

Yes! Team Mode is specifically designed for cooperative play. In Normal Mode, you can still ally with other players informally. Having teammates protect your base while you AFK or gather resources is a proven long-term survival strategy.

What happens when a season resets?

Your level resets to 0 but your previous season's score is archived. You keep your season medal showing your historical achievement. All in-game progress resets, so everyone starts on equal footing at the beginning of each season.

Which biome has the best resources?

Each biome offers unique resources: Forest has basic materials, Winter has Diamonds and Amethysts, Lava has Reidite, and Desert has Emeralds. The Winter cave is especially valuable as it contains both Diamonds and Amethysts in the same location. Late-game progression requires visiting all biomes.

Swords or spears?

Swords deal more damage and are better for PvE grinding. Spears have longer range, making them better for PvP and for fighting fast mobs where first-hit advantage matters. Most experienced players carry both and switch based on the situation.
